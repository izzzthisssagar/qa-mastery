A **test plan** documents the *what, how, who, and when* of a test effort. It
turns "we'll test it" into a shared, reviewable agreement. A **test strategy** is
the higher-level, often organisation-wide, statement of testing approach the plan
draws on.

## What a test plan covers

- **Scope** — what's in, and (just as important) what's **out**.
- **Objectives** — what "done" and "good enough" mean here.
- **Approach** — levels (unit/integration/system) and types (functional,
  performance, security) in play.
- **Entry / exit criteria** — when testing can start, and when it can stop.
- **Resources** — people, environments, tools, data.
- **Schedule** — milestones and estimates.
- **Risks & mitigations** — what could derail testing, and the plan B.
- **Deliverables** — cases, reports, metrics.

## Entry & exit criteria

These are the plan's teeth. **Entry:** the build deploys, the environment is up,
smoke passes. **Exit:** all planned cases run, no open blockers/criticals, the
agreed coverage met. Without explicit exit criteria, "are we done testing?"
becomes a matter of opinion.

## Keep it living

The IEEE 829 layout is a useful checklist, not a mandate. On an agile team a
one-page test plan per release — scope, risks, exit criteria — beats a 40-page
document nobody reads. The value is the *conversation and alignment*, not the page count.
