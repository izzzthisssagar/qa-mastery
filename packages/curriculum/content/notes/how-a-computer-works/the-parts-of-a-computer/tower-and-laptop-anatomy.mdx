<Hook>
Every app you will ever test runs on a physical box somewhere — and the first time a
teammate says *"just plug it into the tower"* or *"check if the laptop is on the charger"*,
you want to know exactly what they mean. This topic gives you the names of the parts, so
hardware stops being a mystery.
</Hook>

<Callout type="analogy">
A computer is like a **restaurant kitchen**. The tower (or laptop body) is the kitchen
itself; inside it there's a head chef (the CPU), counter space for work in progress (RAM),
and a pantry for storage (the disk). The monitor, keyboard and mouse are the serving
window — how orders come in and dishes go out. Today we only walk around the kitchen from
the *outside*; we meet the chef in the next chapter.
</Callout>

## The two shapes a computer comes in

Almost every computer you'll meet at work is one of two shapes:

- A **desktop** — a separate box (the <Term define="The upright case that holds the actual computer: motherboard, CPU, RAM, disks and power supply.">tower</Term>) plus a monitor, keyboard and mouse that plug into it.
- A **laptop** — the same parts, shrunk and folded into one portable case with the screen, keyboard and <Term define="The touch-sensitive rectangle below a laptop keyboard that moves the pointer — the built-in mouse.">trackpad</Term> built in.

The important idea: **they are the same machine inside.** A laptop is a desktop that
learned to travel. Everything you learn about one applies to the other.

## Walk around a desktop tower

Stand a tower in front of you and you'll find, from top to bottom:

- **Power button** — usually on the front or top, marked with the ⏻ symbol.
- **Front ports** — a couple of <Term define="Universal Serial Bus — the rectangular plug used by keyboards, mice, drives and phones.">USB</Term> ports and a headphone jack, put there for convenience.
- **The back panel** — where everything permanent plugs in: power cable, monitor cable, network cable, and more USB. (We tour every port type in *Ports & cables*, two topics from now.)
- **Air vents and fans** — the machine breathes. Never block them; heat is a computer's worst enemy.

Everything else — the actual computing — happens *inside* the case, on parts you can't
see right now: the motherboard, CPU, RAM and disk. Next chapter opens the case.

## Walk around a laptop

- **Lid & screen** — open it and the machine usually wakes by itself.
- **Keyboard & trackpad** — built in; an external mouse plugs into USB if you prefer.
- **Sides** — that's where the ports live on a laptop: charger socket, USB, maybe HDMI and a headphone jack.
- **Underside** — air vents and, on many models, the battery. Same rule: don't block the vents (testing from a bed with the laptop on a blanket genuinely overheats machines).
- **Charger (power brick)** — the one part beginners forget is *part of the computer*. No charger, and the machine lives only as long as its battery.

<FirstTime title="First time? Identify the machine in front of you">
1. Look at the computer you're using right now. Is it a laptop (one folded unit) or a desktop (separate tower + monitor)?
2. Find the **power button** and just locate it — no need to press anything.
3. Count the **USB ports** you can see. Laptops: check both sides. Desktops: check front *and* back.
4. Find the **air vents**. On a laptop they're usually on the bottom or back edge; on a tower, the back and sides.
5. Find where **power** comes in: the charger socket on a laptop, the thick cable at the top back of a tower.

That's it — you can now point at every external part of your machine and name it.
</FirstTime>

<WhereToCheck>
You can verify what hardware you have without opening the case — the operating system
will tell you:

- **Windows:** Settings → System → About shows the machine's name, CPU and RAM.
- **macOS:**  menu → About This Mac.
- **Any laptop:** the model number is printed on the sticker on the underside — that's the string to search when you need drivers or a manual.

This "let the system tell you" habit is your first taste of a core QA skill: don't
guess about the environment, **check it**. Bug reports say "on what machine" for a reason.
</WhereToCheck>

<Callout type="mistake">
Calling the **monitor** "the computer". The screen is only a display — the computer is
the tower (or the laptop body). If a teammate asks you to "restart the computer" and you
only switch the monitor off and on, nothing restarts. It's the classic beginner mix-up;
now it's one you'll never make.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip">
Testers meet hardware in bug reports. "Works on my machine" often decodes to a hardware
difference: less RAM, a different screen size, no dedicated graphics. Knowing the part
names lets you write — and read — an environment line like
<em>"Reproduced on a 14-inch laptop, 8 GB RAM, external monitor attached."</em>
</Callout>

<Challenge>
Write down, for the machine you're using right now: **(1)** desktop or laptop, **(2)** where
its power button is, **(3)** how many USB ports it has, **(4)** where the air vents are, and
**(5)** its exact model (from About This Mac / Settings → About / the sticker underneath).
Congratulations — you just documented a test environment for the first time. Keep that
note; you'll reuse the habit in every bug report you ever write.
</Challenge>
